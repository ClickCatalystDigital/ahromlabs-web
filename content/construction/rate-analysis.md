## What is rate analysis?

Rate analysis works out the cost of one unit of an item of work from what goes into it: the quantity of each
material, the labour by trade, tools and plant, then water charges and the contractor's overheads and profit. The
result is the rate a contractor quotes against a [BOQ](/systems/bill-of-quantities) line and the rate an owner checks
it against.

## What goes into a rate?

| Component | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Materials | Quantity per unit (with wastage) × rate delivered to site |
| Labour | Mason, helper, carpenter, bar bender and so on, in man-days per unit × wage |
| Tools and plant (T&P) | Mixers, vibrators, scaffolding, shuttering hire and wear, as a lump sum or per unit |
| Sundries | Small items not worth measuring |
| Water charges | Commonly 1% of the cost, in CPWD-style analyses |
| Overheads and profit | Commonly 15% of the cost, in CPWD-style analyses |

The analysis is usually for a round quantity (10 m³ of concrete, 10 m² of plaster) and divided down to the unit.

## Can you show a worked example?

An illustrative analysis for 1 m³ of M20 nominal mix concrete (1:1.5:3) for a slab, excluding reinforcement and
shuttering. Quantities and rates are examples, not CPWD's figures: use your own and the current schedule.

| Item | Quantity | Rate (₹) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cement | 8.0 bags | 400 per bag | 3,200 |
| Coarse sand | 0.44 m³ | 2,800 per m³ | 1,232 |
| Stone aggregate 20 mm | 0.88 m³ | 2,200 per m³ | 1,936 |
| Mason | 0.17 day | 1,000 | 170 |
| Beldar (helper) | 2.0 days | 750 | 1,500 |
| Mixer and vibrator hire, fuel | | | 300 |
| **Cost of materials, labour and T&P** | | | **8,338** |
| Water charges at 1% | | | 83 |
| Overheads and profit at 15% | | | 1,251 |
| **Rate per m³** | | | **9,672** |

Change any input and the rate changes with it, which is why the analysis, not just the rate, should be kept with the
estimate.

## What are CPWD's Schedule of Rates and cost index?

CPWD issues a Schedule of Rates (the Delhi Schedule of Rates, DSR) for standard items and revises it periodically:
"The SORs are ordinarily revised once in two years." Between revisions, a cost index adjusts the schedule for price
movement; the Works Manual defines it as "An indicator of the average cost movement over time of a fixed basket of
representative goods and services related to execution of works" and says "The cost index shall be issued as on 1st
April and 1st October every year." A CPWD detailed estimate is "based on the rates given in theSOR for those items
covered by it and on market rates for the remaining items".

## How are items not in the schedule rated?

From a market rate analysis: current quotations for the materials, local wages, and the same overheads and profit,
with the quotations kept as evidence. CPWD's item-rate tender form is used where "most of the items are non-scheduled
or specialized items and detailed analysis of rates is not available". The same applies to extra items and
substitutions during the job: price them by analysis and get the rate agreed before the work, or it becomes a
dispute at the final bill. See [RA bills](/construction/ra-bills) and the
[price escalation clause](/construction/price-escalation-clause).

## What wastage allowances are used?

Rate analyses add wastage to material quantities: typically a few percent for cement and steel and more for bricks
and tiles, depending on the item and handling. CPWD's contract sets allowances for comparing theoretical with actual
consumption of key materials; see [material consumption vs BOQ](/construction/material-consumption-vs-boq). Use your
own site history where you have it: a rate that assumes 2% wastage on a site that loses 6% is wrong by the
difference.

## How do you analyse a rate for plastering?

A second illustrative example: 10 m² of 12 mm cement plaster 1:6 on brick walls. Quantities and rates are examples.

| Item | Quantity | Rate (₹) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cement mortar 1:6, wet volume 0.12 m³, about 0.15 m³ dry with wastage: cement | 0.62 bag | 400 | 248 |
| Sand | 0.13 m³ | 2,800 | 364 |
| Mason | 0.8 day | 1,000 | 800 |
| Beldar | 1.0 day | 750 | 750 |
| Scaffolding and sundries | | | 60 |
| **Cost** | | | **2,222** |
| Water at 1% | | | 22 |
| Overheads and profit at 15% | | | 333 |
| **Rate per 10 m²** | | | **2,577** |
| **Rate per m²** | | | **258** |

The labour figures drive most plaster rates; a site where masons do 15 m² a day instead of 12.5 is cheaper by the
difference, which is why productivity belongs in the analysis rather than a single blended rate.

## When does a contractor need its own rate analysis?

- **Tendering on an item rate contract**, to know which rates are low and which carry the margin.
- **Pricing extra and substituted items** during the job, with the analysis attached to the claim.
- **Checking a subcontractor's quote** for a trade.
- **Escalation claims**, where the price of a material in the analysis moves; see the
  [price escalation clause](/construction/price-escalation-clause).

Keep the analysis in a sheet where the material and labour rates sit in one place and every item refers to them, so
updating the price of cement updates every rate that uses it.

## How does rate analysis connect to the site?

The analysis says how much cement, sand and steel each unit should take. Comparing it with what the site actually
consumed for the measured work is how a contractor finds wastage and theft. See
[material consumption vs BOQ](/construction/material-consumption-vs-boq). In our construction demo each BOQ line can
carry a norm (so much of one material per unit of work, the same quantities a rate analysis uses), and the site's
Material tab compares expected use with what was actually issued, flagging anything more than 10% out. See
[software for construction companies](/industries/construction-company).
