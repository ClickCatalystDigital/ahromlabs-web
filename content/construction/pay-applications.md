## What goes into a pay application?

A pay application is the contractor's monthly bill against progress: for every line of the schedule of values it
shows the work completed before this period, the work completed this period, materials stored, the total to date,
the [retainage](/construction/retainage) held, and the amount now due. US construction contracts are usually paid
this way rather than on milestones.

The American Institute of Architects (AIA) publishes the most used forms, G702 and G703. They are paid, copyrighted
documents (AIA offers free partial samples of [G702](https://aiacontracts.com/samples/aia-g702-1992-pay-application-sample.pdf) and [G703](https://aiacontracts.com/samples/aia-g703-1992-continuation-sheet-sample.pdf)); many owners and general contractors use their own form with the same
columns and arithmetic.

## What is the difference between G702 and G703?

G702 is the summary: one page of totals that the contractor signs to apply for payment and the architect signs to
certify it. G703 is the continuation sheet: one row per line of the schedule of values. [AIA's instructions](https://help.aiacontracts.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500009308302-Instructions-G703-1992-Continuation-Sheet) have the
contractor fill in G703 first and carry its totals to G702, then sign G702 and send both to the architect.

| | G702, Application and Certificate for Payment | G703, Continuation Sheet |
|---|---|---|
| What it holds | Contract sum, change orders, totals, retainage, previous certificates, payment due | Each schedule of values line with its scheduled value, work and stored materials |
| Who signs | Contractor (application, notarized) and architect (certificate) | Nobody separately; it is attached to G702 |
| Change orders | Summarized in a change order box and line 2 | Usually listed as their own lines |

## What is a schedule of values, and who prepares it?

The schedule of values is the contract sum broken into lines (general conditions, concrete, steel, electrical and
so on) that every pay application bills against. The contractor prepares it and submits it to the architect at the
start of the job, as the general conditions require; AIA [doesn't publish](https://help.aiacontracts.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500009387461-summary-g702-1992-application-and-certificate-for-payment) a standard schedule of values form. AIA's
instructions say the breakdown, whether by sections of work or by subcontractor, should stay consistent through the
project.

Its total must equal the original contract sum. Owners and architects watch for **front-loading**: early items
valued high so the contractor is paid ahead of the work. Agree the lines and values before the first application;
changing them later is harder than getting them right.

## How do you fill in G703, column by column?

| Column | Holds | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| A | Item number | |
| B | Description of work | |
| C | Scheduled value | Starts at the original contract sum; change orders are usually added as their own lines |
| D | Work completed, from previous application | Previous D plus previous E |
| E | Work completed, this period | Includes stored material from earlier periods now installed |
| F | Materials presently stored | Not in D or E; value moves to E as the material is installed |
| G | Total completed and stored to date | D + E + F, with % complete (G ÷ C) |
| H | Balance to finish | C − G |
| I | Retainage, if the rate varies by line | Only needed where variable retainage applies |

A worked G703 on a made-up $400,000 contract with one approved $20,000 change order, at the end of month three:

| Item | C Scheduled | D Previous | E This period | F Stored | G To date | % | H Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 General conditions | $40,000 | $12,000 | $4,000 | | $16,000 | 40% | $24,000 |
| 2 Concrete | $120,000 | $60,000 | $30,000 | | $90,000 | 75% | $30,000 |
| 3 Structural steel | $140,000 | $18,000 | $20,000 | $10,000 | $48,000 | 34% | $92,000 |
| 4 Electrical | $100,000 | | $6,000 | | $6,000 | 6% | $94,000 |
| CO 1 Added canopy | $20,000 | | | | | 0% | $20,000 |
| **Total** | **$420,000** | **$90,000** | **$60,000** | **$10,000** | **$160,000** | **38%** | **$260,000** |

## How is retainage calculated on a pay application?

Retainage is a percentage of the work completed (columns D + E) plus a percentage of the materials stored (column
F); the two rates can differ if the contract says so. G702 then takes it off the total to date and deducts what was
certified before. With 10% on both, the totals above go onto G702 like this:

| G702 line | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Original contract sum | | $400,000 |
| 2 Net change by change orders | | $20,000 |
| 3 Contract sum to date | Line 1 ± 2 | $420,000 |
| 4 Total completed and stored to date | G703 column G | $160,000 |
| 5a Retainage on completed work | 10% × (D + E) = 10% × $150,000 | $15,000 |
| 5b Retainage on stored material | 10% × F = 10% × $10,000 | $1,000 |
| 5 Total retainage | 5a + 5b | $16,000 |
| 6 Total earned less retainage | Line 4 − line 5 | $144,000 |
| 7 Less previous certificates for payment | Line 6 of the last certificate: $90,000 − $9,000 | $81,000 |
| 8 **Current payment due** | Line 6 − line 7 | **$63,000** |
| 9 Balance to finish, including retainage | Line 3 − line 6 | $276,000 |

Check: this period's $60,000 of work plus $10,000 stored is $70,000; less 10% is $63,000. If the contract retains
nothing on stored material, line 5b is zero and line 8 becomes $64,000. When retainage is reduced (for example at
50% complete), line 5 falls and the release shows up as extra payment on line 8, so the reduction must be written in
the contract or approved in writing first.

## Can you bill for stored materials?

Usually yes, if the contract allows it and you can prove the material exists and is yours to sell to the owner.
Stored material goes in column F at its value; it is not deducted when the owner pays for it, only moved to column E
as it is installed. Owners and lenders typically ask for the supplier's invoice, photos, proof of insurance, and for
material stored off site, a bill of sale or other proof of title.

On federal jobs, [FAR 52.232-5](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-5) lets the contracting officer count material delivered to the site. Material held
elsewhere counts only if the contract specifically allows it and the contractor gives satisfactory evidence that it
has acquired title and that the material will be used on the contract.

## Does a G702 need to be notarized?

The G702 form has a notary block under the contractor's signature, and [AIA's instructions](https://help.aiacontracts.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500009308242-Instructions-G702-1992-Application-and-Certificate-for-Payment) say the contractor should
sign it and have it notarized. Whether a notarized signature is legally required depends on the contract and the
owner; many owners and lenders insist on it, some accept electronic signatures without it. Check the contract's
payment section before the first application rather than finding out when it is returned.

## What is a pencil draft, and when is a pay application due?

A pencil draft (or pencil copy) is an unsigned draft of the pay application sent to the owner's representative or
architect a few days before the real one, so they can agree the percentages before anything is notarized. It is
common practice, not something the AIA forms require. The contract sets the period the application covers, the day
it is due each month and how long the owner has to pay; a late or rejected application usually waits for the next
cycle, which can cost a month of cash.

On federal jobs the due date is set by [FAR 52.232-27](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/52.232-27): a progress payment is due 14 days after the designated billing
office receives a proper payment request, and a request that isn't proper must be returned within 7 days with the
reasons.

## Who approves a pay application, and can they approve less?

The architect (or the owner's representative where there is no architect) checks the application against the work
on site and certifies the amount due, which may be less than applied for. AIA's instructions say the architect then
initials every figure changed on G702 and G703 and attaches an explanation; the owner pays the amount certified.
Lenders often want [lien waivers](/construction/lien-waivers) from the contractor and from the subcontractors paid
from the previous application before releasing money.

On federal jobs, FAR 52.232-5 sets the rules: progress payments monthly on estimates of work accomplished, approved
by the contracting officer, with an itemization of the amounts by element of work, a listing of each subcontractor's
work, subcontract value and previous payments, and a certification with every request or payment isn't made. The
certification says, among other things, that subcontractors and suppliers have been paid from previous payments.

## Why do pay applications get rejected?

Most rejections come from a handful of causes:

- **The arithmetic doesn't tie.** G703 totals don't match G702, or line 7 isn't line 6 of the last certificate.
- **Percent complete ahead of the site.** The architect sees less work in place than billed.
- **Stored material without proof.** No invoice, title or insurance, or it's off site without contract approval.
- **Unapproved change orders billed.** Only executed [change orders](/construction/change-orders) belong in column C.
- **Missing lien waivers** for the previous payment, from the contractor or subcontractors.
- **Missing signature, notarization or certification**, or the wrong period.
- **A changed schedule of values** that no longer matches the approved one.

Each of these is cheaper to prevent with a pencil draft than to fix after the application is returned.

## What should pay application software do?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Hold the schedule of values per job, locked once approved, with change orders added as their own lines.
- Take quantities or percentages from site progress, not typed in at month-end.
- Carry each certified application forward, so column D and line 7 always match what was certified, including any
  amount the architect cut.
- Compute retainage per line, with separate rates for work and stored material, and its reduction and release.
- Move stored material to installed when it is used, without double counting.
- Track the lien waivers owed per application, and block payment of a subcontractor until theirs is in.
- Produce the application in the owner's format, and keep each submitted and certified version.
- Feed the same figures to the [WIP schedule](/construction/wip-schedule), so billing and cost are compared per job.
