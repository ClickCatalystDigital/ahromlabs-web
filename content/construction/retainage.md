## What is retainage in construction?

Retainage (also called retention) is part of each progress payment that the owner holds back until the job is
finished, as security that the contractor completes the work and fixes defects. Each
[pay application](/construction/pay-applications) bills the work completed and stored to date, takes off the
retainage percentage, and the balance is paid. The amount held builds up over the job and is paid at the end. The
general contractor usually holds the same percentage from each subcontractor, so retainage runs down the whole chain.

## How much retainage is normal?

Often 5% or 10% of each payment, as the contract sets it, and less where the state caps it. Some contracts lower the
rate later in the job; federal rules allow the amount withheld to be adjusted as the contract nears completion. Agree
the rate before the job starts and read the contract's retainage clause before you sign it.

A made-up $400,000 job at 10%, at the end of month three:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Work completed to date | $160,000 |
| Materials stored on site | $8,000 |
| Total completed and stored | $168,000 |
| Less retainage at 10% | $16,800 |
| Total earned less retainage | $151,200 |
| Less previous certificates | $108,000 |
| **Current payment due** | **$43,200** |

Over the whole job, $40,000 waits for the end, often months after the work it covers. The same job under a California
private contract signed in 2026 could hold at most 5%: $8,400 at this point and $20,000 over the job.

## Which states cap retainage, and at what rate?

California, New York, Texas and Florida each have rules, in different shapes:

| State | Private jobs | Public jobs | Release |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 5% of each payment and 5% of the contract price in total, for contracts signed from 1 January 2026 (Civil Code 8811); not on residential projects of four stories or fewer that aren't mixed-use | 5% (Public Contract Code 7201) | Owner pays within 45 days after completion (Civil Code 8812) |
| New York | 5% of the contract sum on projects of $150,000 or more (General Business Law 756, 756-c, 757) | Not covered by these sections | Within 30 days after final approval; 1% a month interest if late |
| Texas | The owner must reserve 10% until 30 days after completion, for lien claimants (Property Code 53.101); the contract's own retainage is separate | 10% under $5 million, 5% at $5 million or more (Government Code 2252.032) | As the contract says; payment requests are due within 35 days (Property Code 28.002) |
| Florida | As the contract says | 5% of each payment on contracts over $200,000 (Statutes 255.078) | Same time limits as any payment request |

For any other state, read that state's own statute for where the project is. In New York, a private contract clause
asking for more than 5% is void under General Business Law 757.

## What are the rules on federal jobs?

Federal contracting officers pay in full when progress is satisfactory and may hold back up to 10% only when it isn't
(FAR 52.232-5). FAR 32.103 adds that retainage "should not be used as a substitute for good contract management". At
substantial completion the officer keeps only what protects the Government and releases the rest; retained amounts are
due as the contract says or, if it's silent, 30 days after the officer approves their release (FAR 52.232-27).

## When must retainage be released?

When the contract says, and no later than the state's deadline. In California the owner must pay the retention within
45 days after the work is completed; in New York within 30 days after final approval of the work; on federal jobs
within 30 days of the contracting officer approving release when the contract is silent. Release usually needs
paperwork first: final [lien waivers](/construction/lien-waivers), closeout documents and a finished punch list.

## Can the owner keep retainage for punch-list items or a dispute?

Only a limited amount. In California, if there is a good-faith dispute, the owner may withhold no more than 150% of the
disputed amount from final payment. On the $400,000 job with $20,000 retained and $3,000 of punch-list work disputed,
the owner may hold $4,500 and must pay the other $15,500 within 45 days of completion.

## Can a GC hold more retainage from a sub than the owner holds from the GC?

Not in California or New York: a subcontract's retainage percentage can't exceed the one in the owner's contract with
the GC. On federal jobs the prime contractor must pay subs for satisfactory work within 7 days of being paid (31 U.S.C.
3905), and the subcontract may permit retainage on the sub as agreed.

## What happens if the owner pays late?

The owner owes interest or a penalty at the rate the state sets. In Texas an unpaid amount bears interest at 1.5% a
month, so a $43,200 payment made a month late costs the owner $648. In California an owner who wrongfully withholds a
progress payment owes 2% a month; in New York, retainage released late carries 1% a month.

## Does retainage apply to stored materials?

Yes on a standard pay application: retainage is taken on the total completed and stored, so stored materials carry it
too. Texas caps the rate on public jobs per line of the schedule of values, "including materials and equipment
delivered on site to be installed".

## How should a contractor track retainage?

Per job and per subcontractor, from the pay applications, never as one figure in the books:

- **Retainage receivable** is money you've billed and earned but can't collect yet. It sits inside "billed to date" on
  the [WIP schedule](/construction/wip-schedule); cash collected doesn't include it.
- **Retainage payable** is what you hold from each sub, released on the same terms once the owner pays you.
- **Release dates**: completion, final approval and the state's deadline, so a late release is chased with interest.
- **Release paperwork**: lien waivers, closeout documents and the punch list, tracked per job.

The [construction demo](/industries/construction-company)'s US build holds retainage on every pay application, on
work completed and stored materials, at a rate set per application. It doesn't yet bill a retainage release or track
retainage held from subcontractors; those are parts we'd build for a contractor.
