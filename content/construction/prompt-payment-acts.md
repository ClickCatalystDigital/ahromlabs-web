## What is a prompt payment act?

A prompt payment act is a law that sets how quickly an owner must pay its contractor, and each contractor its subs
and suppliers, and what is owed when a payment is late. The federal government has one for its own contracts, and
many states have their own, some for public jobs only and some for private jobs too. The contract usually sets the
billing cycle; the act puts a ceiling on how long payment can take and adds interest or a penalty after it.

## How fast must the federal government pay a contractor?

Within 14 days after the designated billing office receives a proper payment request for a progress payment, under
FAR 52.232-27 and the Prompt Payment Act (31 U.S.C. 3903); interest runs on an approved payment left unpaid after
that. The prime must pay its subs for satisfactory work within 7 days of being paid (31 U.S.C. 3905), and the
subcontract must say so.

## What are the deadlines in Texas, California, New York and Florida?

| State | Owner pays the contractor | Contractor pays its subs | If late |
|---|---|---|---|
| Texas, private | 35 days after a written payment request (Property Code 28.002) | 7 days after receiving the owner's payment | 1.5% a month (28.004) |
| Texas, public | Overdue on the 31st day after the later of delivery, completion or invoice (Government Code 2251.021) | 10 days after receiving payment (2251.022) | As chapter 2251 sets |
| California, private | 30 days after notice demanding payment, unless agreed otherwise in writing (Civil Code 8800) | 7 days after each progress payment, unless agreed otherwise in writing (Business and Professions Code 7108.5) | 2% a month, owner and contractor alike |
| New York, private, $150,000 and up | Approve or reject within 12 business days; pay within 30 days of approval (General Business Law 756-a) | 7 days after receiving good funds, once documents and waivers are in | As article 35-E sets |
| Florida, private | When the contract says, once the request, payment from above and waivers are in (Statutes 715.12) | Same rule, once the contractor has been paid for that work | Statutory rate plus 12% a year, from the 14th day after due |
| Florida, public | Undisputed portion by the contract date or 20 business days after the request, whichever is earlier (255.073) | 10 days after the contractor is paid; 7 days for a sub paying its subs | As the statute sets |

These are the main payment deadlines only; each act has its own exceptions, definitions and project thresholds.

## Can an owner hold back part of a payment?

Yes, for a good-faith dispute, but only so much. In California an owner may withhold no more than 150% of the
disputed amount from a progress payment, and a contractor the same from its sub. In Texas the withholding in a
good-faith dispute is capped at the difference in dispute (110% of it on detached homes up to a quadruplex). In New
York an owner who rejects part of an invoice must say in writing which items aren't approved and why.

## How is interest on a late payment worked out?

At the statute's rate, from the day after the payment was due. A $43,200 progress payment on a Texas private job,
due 35 days after the request and paid a month late, carries 1.5% for that month: $648. In California the same
amount wrongfully withheld for a month costs the owner 2%, $864. Florida's private-job rate is the statutory judgment
rate plus 12% a year, starting 14 days after the payment was due.

## Can a contractor stop work if it isn't paid?

In some states, after notice. In Texas a contractor or sub may suspend work on the 10th day after giving the owner
(and the lender, where required) written notice that an undisputed payment hasn't been received; that right doesn't
apply to single-family homes up to a quadruplex or to public jobs. In New York a contract clause saying a party can't
suspend work when the other doesn't pay promptly is void (General Business Law 757).

## Do prompt payment rules cover retainage?

Usually, with their own deadlines. In California the owner pays retention within 45 days after completion and the
contractor passes each sub's share on within 10 days of receiving it (Civil Code 8812, 8814); in New York retainage is
released within 30 days of final approval. See [retainage](/construction/retainage) for each state's caps.

## Can the contract override the prompt payment law?

Partly, and less than most contractors think. California's 30-day and 7-day rules give way to a different written
agreement; Texas makes an attempted waiver of chapter 28 void, apart from a longer period allowed on single-family
homes; in New York the contract governs except where the article says otherwise, and payment terms that differ from
its payment sections are void. Read the contract's payment clause against the state's act before signing.

## What should a contractor track to enforce prompt payment?

The dates the law counts from, per pay application: when the request was delivered, when it was approved, when the
owner paid, and when each sub was paid from it. With those, the system can flag a late payment, work out the
interest, and show that you paid your own subs on time, which matters when you are the one being chased.

- The date each [pay application](/construction/pay-applications) was submitted and approved.
- Payments received against each application, and payments made to each sub from it.
- The state of the project, so the right deadline and rate apply.
- Disputed items, with the written reason, kept apart from undisputed amounts.
