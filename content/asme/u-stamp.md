## The steps

ASME's informational handbook sets out the route. In the order a shop usually does it:

1. **Decide the scope.** Which designator (U for Division 1 vessels), shop only or field work too, and any limits. The scope drives everything else; see [ASME stamps](/asme/stamps).
2. **Buy the Code books.** Current editions of every section the scope uses (ASME publishes the list per certificate). Copies aren't accepted.
3. **Write the quality control manual.** In English, covering every element the Code requires for the scope. See [the quality control manual](/asme/quality-control-manual).
4. **Sign an inspection agreement** with an ASME-accredited Authorized Inspection Agency. The agency's [Authorized Inspector](/asme/authorized-inspector) will work with you on every stamped job.
5. **Apply** through ASME's CA Connect, with the deposit and fees, and return the signed ASME Accreditation/Certification Agreement.
6. **Prepare the demonstration.** Real Code work, a mock-up, or both, run through your system from drawing to data report: design calculations, material receipt and [MTRs](/asme/mtr), [WPS and PQR](/asme/wps-pqr-wpq), welder qualifications, NDE, a [hydrostatic test](/asme/hydrostatic-test) and a completed [data report](/asme/data-reports).
7. **The joint review.** A team leader (the ASME Designee) and a representative of your inspection agency audit the manual and watch the demonstration. They recommend issue to ASME, or list what must be fixed first.
8. **Certificate and stamp issued.** Valid for three years.

## Who leads the review

The ASME Designee is appointed by ASME, by the jurisdiction, or by the National Board, depending on where the shop is. The handbook names the US states where the jurisdiction appoints (Alaska, Delaware, Hawaii, Louisiana, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oklahoma, Texas, Washington), says ASME appoints for Idaho, Wyoming, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands and all Canadian provinces, and the National Board for the other states. See [US and Canadian boiler laws](/asme/state-boiler-laws).

## What it costs

ASME publishes its fees in its Price Guide; they change, so check the current one. The full cost to budget is more than ASME's fee:

- ASME's application and certificate fees, the review deposit, and the Designee's travel when ASME appoints;
- the inspection agency's fees for the review and then for every job it inspects;
- the Code books;
- staff time to write the manual and run the demonstration, and the demonstration item itself;
- any consultant who helps write the manual.

## How long it takes

There's no official timeline. The pace is usually set by the shop: writing a manual that matches how the shop really works, and getting a demonstration job through every step. Plan in months, not weeks, and book the review only when a full job can be shown end to end.

## Keeping it

The certificate lasts three years. Renewal is another joint review, and ASME must receive the renewal application at least six months before expiry. A shop with work in progress at expiry can ask for one six-month extension to finish that work.

## In India

The route is the same for a shop in India, and many Indian fabricators hold U and S certificates for export work. IBR 2026 lets an Indian Inspecting Authority inspect an export boiler to a foreign code; see [buying ASME equipment from India](/asme/buying-from-india) and [ASME vs IBR 2026](/asme/asme-vs-ibr).
