## Do interior designers have to file 1099s?

Yes, for people they pay for services who aren't employees. [The IRS](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec) says a payment is reported as nonemployee
compensation on Form 1099-NEC when four conditions are met: it went to someone who is not your employee, it was for
services in the course of your business, it went to an individual, partnership, estate or, in some cases, a
corporation, and you paid that payee at least $2,000 in the year. A design firm meets all four with the independent
installers, workrooms, upholsterers, painters and art handlers it pays directly.

## What is the 1099 threshold for 2026?

$2,000, up from $600. The IRS instructions say that for tax years beginning after 2025 the minimum threshold for
these information returns, and for backup withholding on the same payments, rose to $2,000 and may be adjusted for
inflation from 2027. Payments made in 2026 are the first ones measured against it; the forms are due in January 2027.

| Paid in a calendar year to one independent installer | Before 2026 | From 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| $1,500 | Form 1099-NEC ($600 or more) | No form |
| $2,000 | Form 1099-NEC | Form 1099-NEC |
| $8,400 | Form 1099-NEC | Form 1099-NEC |

The threshold is per payee for the year, so add up every payment to the same person across all projects.

## Which of a design firm's payees need a 1099-NEC?

| Payee | 1099-NEC? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Independent installer or white-glove crew (a sole proprietor or LLC taxed as one) | Yes, at $2,000+ | Services, not an employee |
| Drapery workroom or upholsterer, unincorporated | Yes, on the whole bill, at $2,000+ | Fabric they supply as part of the job is usually incidental to the service |
| Furniture vendor or showroom | No | Payments for merchandise aren't reported |
| Freight carrier, receiving warehouse storage | No | Freight and storage are listed as not reportable |
| Installer that is an S or C corporation | Generally no | Payments to corporations are generally exempt |
| Anyone paid by credit card or a payment network | No (for those payments) | The card company reports them on Form 1099-K |
| An architect or engineer you engage | Yes, at $2,000+ (unless a corporation) | Listed by the IRS as professional service fees |

On the workroom row: the IRS says to report payment for services including parts or materials used, if supplying
them was incidental to the service. A workroom that sells you finished draperies made from its own fabric is closer
to a merchandise vendor; one that sews your client's fabric is a service. Where it isn't clear, ask your accountant
before January.

## When are 1099-NECs due?

By January 31, to the IRS and to the payee. The IRS instructions say section 6071(c) requires Form 1099-NEC to be
filed on or before January 31, on paper or electronically; if the date falls on a weekend or holiday, the next
business day. Form 1099-MISC (for rents and some other payments) has later IRS filing dates. The bottleneck is
never the form: it is having every payee's taxpayer ID before you pay them.

## How do you get a vendor's tax ID?

With Form W-9, before the first payment. The IRS's 1099 instructions point to Form W-9 for getting a payee's taxpayer
identification number, and its independent contractor FAQs cover the same questions. Make the W-9 part of opening any
new installer, workroom or subcontractor, and don't release their first check without it; chasing W-9s in January from
people you paid once in March is how firms miss the deadline. The same W-9 tells you whether the payee is a
corporation, which decides whether they need a form at all.

## Does paying by card or Venmo change it?

Yes. The IRS says payments made with a credit card or payment card, and certain third-party network transactions, are
reported on Form 1099-K by the payment settlement entity and are not reported on Form 1099-NEC. If you pay an
installer partly by check and partly by card, only the check payments count toward your $2,000 and your form.

## What should a design firm's system do for 1099s?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Hold a **W-9 per vendor**, with the tax classification (individual, LLC, corporation) and the taxpayer ID.
- Mark each vendor as a **1099 payee** or not, and block payment to a new service vendor without a W-9.
- Record **how each payment was made**: check or ACH count toward the 1099; card payments don't.
- Separate **services from merchandise and freight** on vendor bills, so only services are totalled.
- Total **payments per payee per calendar year**, across every project, against the $2,000 threshold.
- Send vendors, bills and payments to **QuickBooks Online** so the 1099 report there is complete.
