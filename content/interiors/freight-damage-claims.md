## What should you do when furniture arrives damaged?

Write the damage on the delivery receipt before the driver leaves, photograph it, keep the packaging, and then file a
written claim with the carrier. The note on the receipt matters as evidence, but the federal claims regulation is clear
that it isn't a claim: [49 CFR 370.3(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/370.3) says notations of shortage or damage on freight bills, delivery receipts or other
documents do not, standing alone, meet the filing requirements.

| Step | When | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Inspect and note damage on the delivery receipt ("2 cartons crushed, sofa arm split") | At delivery | Receiving warehouse or installer |
| Photograph the piece, the cartons and the labels; keep the packaging | Same day | Receiving warehouse |
| Tell the vendor (it may file, or replace and file itself) | Same day | Purchasing |
| File the written claim with a dollar amount | As soon as the amount is known; within the bill of lading's period | Purchasing |
| Record the claim, the carrier's acknowledgement and its answer | 30 and 120 days | Bookkeeper |

## What makes a freight claim valid?

A written communication to the right carrier, within the time limit, that does three things. Under 49 CFR 370.3(b) it
must contain facts sufficient to identify the shipment, assert the carrier's liability for the loss or damage, and claim
a specified or determinable amount of money. So the claim names the bill of lading or PRO number, the date and the
pieces; says the carrier is liable; and asks for, say, $3,180. A claim for "about $3,000, more or less" isn't one the
carrier may pay until a firm amount is given.

## How long do you have to file a freight claim?

At least 9 months, and at least 2 years to sue. [Federal law (49 U.S.C. 14706(e))](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/49/14706) says a carrier may not set a period of
less than 9 months for filing a claim, or less than 2 years for bringing a lawsuit; the 2 years run from the date the
carrier gives written notice that it has disallowed part of the claim. The bill of lading or the carrier's tariff sets
the actual period, which can be longer but not shorter, so read it for each carrier you use regularly.

## How long does the carrier have to answer?

30 days to acknowledge and 120 days to decide. Under [49 CFR 370.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/370.5) the carrier must acknowledge a proper claim in
writing within 30 days of receiving it (unless it has paid or declined within that time), and under [370.9](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/370.9) it must pay,
decline or make a firm compromise offer in writing within 120 days, and if it can't, tell you the status in writing
then and every 60 days after.

| Day | What should have happened |
|---|---|
| 0 | Claim received by the carrier |
| 30 | Written acknowledgement, with any documents it still needs |
| 120 | Paid, declined, or a firm settlement offer; or a written status |
| Every 60 days after | A written status while the claim is open |

## How much will the carrier pay?

The actual loss, unless the shipment moved at a limited value. 14706 makes the carrier liable for the actual loss or
injury to the property, but it also lets carriers offer rates under which their liability is limited to a value the
shipper declares, or agrees in writing, on the bill of lading. A released value set per pound pays little on a light, expensive
chair. For a $3,180 piece, compare what the released value would pay with
the cost of declaring full value or insuring the shipment, before it ships, not after.

## Who files: the design firm, the vendor or the receiver?

Whoever owns the goods in transit and holds the shipping contract, which depends on the freight terms on the vendor's
acknowledgement. If the vendor arranged and paid the freight and kept ownership until delivery, the vendor usually
files and replaces the piece; if the firm or its receiving warehouse arranged it, the firm files. Settle it per vendor
when the account is opened, and record the freight terms on every purchase order, so nobody is arguing about it with a
broken sofa in the warehouse. [Purchase orders and procurement](/interiors/purchase-orders-and-procurement) covers the
acknowledgement and receiving steps.

## What does a damaged piece do to the client's invoice?

It delays it, and sometimes the deposit. The replacement has its own lead time, the installation date may move, and
the client has paid a deposit for a piece that isn't there. Keep the original order open with the outstanding quantity,
log the claim against it, and decide with the client whether the rest of the room is delivered now or together;
[invoices, deposits and retainers](/interiors/invoices-deposits-and-retainers) covers when the balance is billed.

## What should procurement software track for freight claims?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Record **receipt per PO line**: received date, quantity and condition, as design platforms such as Design Manager do.
- Attach **photos and the signed delivery receipt** to the receipt record.
- Hold the **bill of lading or PRO number**, the carrier and the freight terms on every shipment.
- Open a **claim** against the shipment with the amount, the date filed and who filed it.
- Count the **30- and 120-day** deadlines from the date filed, and flag claims with no answer.
- Keep the **outstanding quantity** open until the replacement arrives, so the PO and the vendor's bill don't close early.
