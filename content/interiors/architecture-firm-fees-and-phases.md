## How do architects charge for a project?

Three common bases, often mixed in one agreement:

| Basis | How it works | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed fee | A set amount for a defined scope | The firm carries scope creep unless additional services are defined |
| Percentage of construction cost | The fee moves with the project's cost | Needs a clear definition of cost and when it is measured |
| Hourly, or a multiple of direct personnel expense | Billed for time spent, sometimes with a cap | The client carries overruns; often used for additional services and early studies |

Reimbursable expenses (printing, travel, permits) and consultants' fees (structural, MEP) are usually billed on top,
sometimes with a markup. The AIA's owner-architect agreements, such as B101, are the common US starting point for
these terms; they are paid documents, so read the version you sign.

## What are the phases of an architecture project?

The traditional five, with typical shares of the fee (they vary by firm and project type):

| Phase | Work | Typical share |
|---|---|---|
| Schematic design (SD) | Concepts, massing, rough plans | 15% |
| Design development (DD) | Systems, materials, detailed plans | 20% |
| Construction documents (CD) | Drawings and specifications for permit and bidding | 40% |
| Bidding or negotiation | Helping the owner select a contractor | 5% |
| Construction administration (CA) | Submittals, RFIs, site visits, pay application review | 20% |

Interior design projects follow a similar arc: concept, design development, documentation, procurement and
installation; see [how to charge for interior design](/interiors/how-to-charge-for-interior-design).

## How is a phase-based fee billed?

Monthly, by percent complete of each phase: the invoice shows each phase's fee, the percent complete, the amount
earned to date, previously billed and due now. It reads much like a contractor's
[pay application](/construction/pay-applications). Additional services, reimbursables and consultant fees are separate
lines. A retainer at signing is credited against the final invoice; see
[invoices, deposits and retainers](/interiors/invoices-deposits-and-retainers).

## How do you know if a phase is profitable?

By budgeting hours (or cost) per phase from the fee, recording time by phase every week, and comparing percent of
budget used with percent complete. A CD phase that has used 80% of its hours at 50% complete will lose money unless
something changes, and the time to change it is now. Track the effective rate too: fee earned divided by hours spent.

## Can you show a phase-based invoice?

A fixed fee of $180,000, split by the typical shares above, billed at the end of month five (figures made up):

| Phase | Fee | % complete | Earned to date | Billed before | Due now |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Schematic design | 27,000 | 100% | 27,000 | 27,000 | 0 |
| Design development | 36,000 | 100% | 36,000 | 28,800 | 7,200 |
| Construction documents | 72,000 | 45% | 32,400 | 14,400 | 18,000 |
| Bidding | 9,000 | 0% | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Construction administration | 36,000 | 0% | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total** | **180,000** | | **95,400** | **70,200** | **25,200** |

Add reimbursables and consultants as separate lines below. A client can check this invoice in a minute, which is
why it gets paid faster than a single line that says "Professional services, May".

## How do additional services work?

Work outside the agreed scope (a second scheme after approval, redesign for a budget cut, extra site visits, an
expanded program) is billed as additional services, usually hourly at agreed rates. The agreement should list what
counts and require the client's approval before the work starts. Log each request with the date, who asked and the
estimate, the same way contractors log change orders; see [change orders](/construction/change-orders). Unbilled
additional services are the commonest reason a profitable-looking fixed fee loses money.

## What numbers should a principal watch?

- **Utilization**: billable hours as a share of total hours, by person.
- **Effective multiplier**: fee earned divided by the direct salary cost of the hours spent.
- **Work in progress**: fee earned but not yet billed, by project.
- **Receivables by age**, and retainers held.
- **Backlog**: signed fees not yet earned, by month, against the team's capacity.

## How should a fee be split between the firm and its consultants?

When the architect carries the consultants (structural, MEP, civil, landscape), their fees sit inside the architect's
fee or are billed through it with a markup for coordination and risk. Set each consultant's share per phase, bill the
client for their percent complete with yours, and pay consultants when the client pays, if the consultant agreements
allow it. Track consultant invoices against their budget the same way as your own hours; a consultant who overruns
construction documents is your overrun too.
