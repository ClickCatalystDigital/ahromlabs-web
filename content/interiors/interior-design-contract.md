## What should an interior design contract include?

Scope, money and product, in writing before work starts. A workable agreement names the services (concept, selections,
purchasing, project administration), how each is paid, the retainer and deposits, who buys the furniture and on what
terms, what happens to orders if the project stops, and how disputes are settled. ASID's 2018 residential forms are a
useful map even if you don't buy them: a letter of agreement with nine schedules, some in several versions, and a blank
schedule for your own terms. ASID says the package should be used with an attorney licensed in your state, and that
the law on design contracts varies from state to state; this page explains the parts, not the wording.

| ASID schedule | Covers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| A, Preliminary project information | The client, property and project | Defines what "the project" is |
| B, Design concept services | Concept and design work | The scope the design fee pays for |
| C, Selection of merchandise | Choosing furnishings | Separates selecting from buying |
| D, Purchasing services (5 versions) | Who buys, and how product is priced | Reseller or agent; markup or fee |
| E, Project administration services | Coordinating vendors, deliveries, installation | Often the unpaid part of the job |
| F, Designer compensation (9 versions) | Fees for design, purchasing, administration | Combined with D, sets the fee structure |
| G, Terms and conditions of sale | Terms for the merchandise the client buys | Where most disputes start |
| H, Termination rights (3 options) | Ending for cause, at will, or with a termination fee | What happens to orders in progress |
| I, Claims and disputes (2 options) | Litigation or arbitration, optional mediation | Cost and speed of a disagreement |
| J, Additional terms; K, supplemental (blank) | Anything else | Your own rules |

## Should the firm resell product or buy as the client's agent?

It is the most important choice in the agreement, because it decides the price, the tax and who carries the risk. ASID's
guide shows both. With the designer as a reseller, the firm buys in its own name and sells to the client, so it can mark
up, it collects the sales tax, and it is the one owed a refund if a vendor fails. With the designer as the client's
purchasing agent, ASID's single-fee version prices merchandise to the client at the vendor's quoted price without any
designer markup; the firm is paid through its fee instead.

| | Firm resells product | Firm buys as the client's agent |
|---|---|---|
| Who the vendor sells to | The firm | The client, through the firm |
| How the firm earns on product | Markup, or a purchasing fee | A purchasing fee, or a fixed fee covering it |
| Who collects sales tax | The firm, on its selling price | Depends on the state and the vendor; ask |
| If a vendor fails or a piece arrives damaged | The firm's claim, then the client's remedy against the firm | The client's claim, with the firm's help |
| What the client sees | The firm's price | The vendor's price and the firm's fee |

ASID's fee combinations also price a purchasing fee as a percentage of the net cost, the established list price or the
vendor's quoted price; [how to charge for interior design](/interiors/how-to-charge-for-interior-design) works three of
them through on a $40,000 order.

## How should fees, the retainer and deposits be written?

As amounts and triggers, not as intentions. Write the design fee (flat, hourly with the rate per role, or per square
foot), what it includes and what is extra; the retainer and what it is applied to (ASID's chapter says it is customarily
paid on signing); the product deposit as a percentage of each approved item, due before the order is placed; when the
balance is due; and the payment terms for each
invoice. Intuit's help on deposits adds a reminder worth putting in the contract review: check your state for any
further rules on customer deposits. [Invoices, deposits and retainers](/interiors/invoices-deposits-and-retainers) has a
worked payment schedule.

## What should the terms of sale for furniture say?

What the client is agreeing to when they approve a piece: that approval authorises the order and the deposit; that
custom, special-order and fabric-cut items can't be cancelled once ordered; who pays restocking fees and return freight;
that lead times are the vendor's estimates; how damage is handled (inspection on receipt, repair or replacement through
the vendor); when title passes; and storage charges if the client isn't ready to take delivery. Each of these is a field
in a procurement system as well as a clause: [purchase orders and procurement](/interiors/purchase-orders-and-procurement)
lists the dates a firm has to track.

## What happens if the client ends the project?

Whatever the termination schedule says, which is why it should cover orders in progress, not only fees. ASID offers
three versions: termination for cause by either side with voluntary termination by the client, the same with a
termination fee, and termination on notice by either side. Whichever you use, say what happens to items already ordered
(the client takes them, or pays the restocking cost), to deposits already paid to vendors, and to design work done but
not yet billed.

## What should contract and billing software hold?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Store the **signed agreement** with the project, and the fee structure as data, not only a PDF.
- Set the **fee type per project**: flat, hourly by role, per square foot, markup or purchasing fee.
- Raise the **retainer** on signing and apply it as the agreement says.
- Require **client approval** before an item can be ordered, and the deposit the agreement sets.
- Mark items as **non-cancellable** once ordered, and hold restocking fees and return freight.
- Record **termination**: what was ordered, what was paid, and the final invoice it produces.
