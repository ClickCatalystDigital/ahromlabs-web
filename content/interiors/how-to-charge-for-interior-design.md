## What are the ways to charge for interior design?

There are four common bases, and most firms combine two of them. ASID's Minnesota chapter lists a fixed (flat) fee
for the whole range of services, an hourly fee for the time actually spent, cost plus (buying furnishings and
services at cost and selling them to the client at cost plus an agreed percentage) and, for larger commercial
projects, a fee per square foot. It is blunt about the number: there is no typical or customary fee.

| Basis | What the client pays for | Works best when | The risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat fee | An agreed sum for the scope | The scope is clear before you start | Scope grows and the fee doesn't |
| Hourly | Time actually spent | Consultations, small or uncertain jobs | The client watches the clock; total is hard to predict |
| Per square foot | Area of the project | Large commercial fit-outs | Area says little about effort on a small, detailed space |
| Cost plus | Product at your cost plus a percentage | Firms that buy and resell product | Clients see your cost; the markup is argued line by line |
| Purchasing fee | A percentage of the product's net cost, billed separately | Product passed through at net | The fee looks like a second charge for the same thing |

## Which combination do most firms use?

Hourly or flat for design, plus a markup or purchasing fee on product. ASID's chapter says cost plus was once the
most widely used structure for residential designers, and that it is now more common to charge an hourly rate for
design services and cost plus for the products the designer buys, or a fixed fee for the entire project.

ASID's own residential contract forms are built the same way: its 2018 user guide pairs a schedule for purchasing
services with a schedule for designer compensation, and the combinations cover a single fixed fee with product at the
designer's net cost and no markup, and a fixed design fee with a separate purchasing fee as a percentage of the net
cost of merchandise, among others. Whichever you pick, it belongs in the [letter of agreement](/interiors/interior-design-contract),
not in an email.

## What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is the percentage added to cost; margin is the share of the selling price that is profit. They are not the
same number, and confusing them is the commonest pricing mistake in a design firm's spreadsheet.

| On $40,000 of product at net cost | 35% markup | 35% margin |
|---|---|---|
| Price to the client | $40,000 × 1.35 = **$54,000** | $40,000 ÷ (1 − 0.35) = **$61,538.46** |
| Gross profit | $14,000 | $21,538.46 |
| As a share of the price | $14,000 ÷ $54,000 = **25.9% margin** | 35% |
| As a share of cost | 35% | $21,538.46 ÷ $40,000 = **53.8% markup** |

To turn a markup into a margin: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup). To price for a target margin: price = cost ÷ (1 −
margin). Agree with the client which one your contract means by "plus 35%"; it should always mean markup on net
cost.

## How does a $40,000 product order work out under each structure?

Take one living-room project: $40,000 of furniture, lighting and fabric at net (trade) cost, the same pieces listing
at $50,000 retail, and 60 hours of design time. The rates below are made up for the example.

| Structure | Design | Product | Client pays (before tax) | Firm's gross profit on product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly + cost plus 35% | 60 h × $175 = $10,500 | $54,000 | $64,500 | $14,000 |
| Hourly + purchasing fee 20% of net | $10,500 | $40,000 + $8,000 fee | $58,500 | $8,000 |
| Flat fee + product at retail | $12,000 | $50,000 (list) | $62,000 | $10,000 (the trade discount) |
| Flat fee, product at net (ASID-style single fee) | $18,000 | $40,000 | $58,000 | $0 |

Two things the table shows. First, cost plus 35% puts the product at $54,000, above its $50,000 retail list, which a
client who checks prices online will notice; [trade discounts](/interiors/trade-accounts-and-designer-discounts) and
how much of them you pass on decide whether a markup is defensible. Second, the single fixed fee only works if it is
set to cover the purchasing and project administration the firm will do, because product earns nothing.

## How much do interior designers mark up furniture?

There is no standard figure, and the sources that cite one disagree; ASID's chapter declines to name a typical fee at
all. What matters more than the percentage is that it is written into the agreement, applied to a defined cost (net
of the trade discount, before or after freight), and shown the same way on every proposal. Firms that want the client
to see the cost use a purchasing fee; firms that don't, price product at a single sell price.

## Does the markup change the sales tax?

Yes, where product is taxable: tax is charged on what the client pays for the product, including your markup. In
California, CDTFA says a decorator's selling price of furnishings should normally be the retail price, cost plus a
reasonable markup. If you bill product at cost and add a separate fee for overhead and profit, CDTFA treats the whole
fee as taxable unless you can clearly show part of it is for nontaxable professional services. State rules differ;
[sales tax for interior designers](/sales-tax/interior-designers-sales-tax) compares New York, California and Texas.

## Can QuickBooks Online add a markup?

Partly. QuickBooks Online Plus and Advanced can mark a bill or expense as billable to a customer, with an optional
default markup rate that can be checked on each expense, and the billable amount is then added to that client's
invoice. That covers passing a vendor's bill through at cost plus a percentage after the bill arrives. It is not a
proposal: the client approves product before you order it, before any bill exists, and a firm wants that approval
organised by room with net cost, markup and sell price on each line. [QuickBooks for interior designers](/interiors/quickbooks-for-interior-designers)
covers where it stops.

## What should pricing software do for a design firm?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Hold **net cost, markup and sell price on every line**, with the markup defaulting per vendor or category and
  editable per line.
- Show **markup and margin** for a line, a room and the whole proposal, labelled so nobody confuses them.
- Price **design time separately** from product, hourly or flat, and keep the two on separate invoice lines.
- Support a **purchasing fee** as a percentage of net, list or vendor's quoted price, as the ASID forms allow.
- Put the **right sales tax on each line**: product taxed with its markup, design fees by the state's rule.
- Compare **quoted against actual**: the vendor's invoice cost against the cost the price was built on.
- Send invoices and bills to **QuickBooks Online** without retyping.
