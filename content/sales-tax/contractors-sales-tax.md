## Do contractors charge sales tax?

Usually not on a construction contract: a contractor who improves real property is generally the consumer of the
materials it installs, so it pays sales tax when it buys them and doesn't add tax to its bill. California's rule says
so directly: "Construction contractors are consumers of materials which they furnish and install in the performance
of construction contracts." The exceptions come from how the contract is written and what is installed, and they
differ by state.

## What is the difference between a lump-sum and a separated contract?

A lump-sum contract has one price for materials and labor together; a separated contract states the price of the
incorporated materials apart from the price of all skill and labor. Texas defines both (34 TAC 3.291), and the
difference decides who owes the tax:

| | Lump-sum contract | Separated contract |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor is | The consumer of the materials | The retailer of the materials |
| Tax on materials | Paid by the contractor to its supplier | Collected by the contractor from the customer |
| Contractor buys materials | Tax paid; no resale certificate for items bought for the job | May give suppliers a resale certificate |
| Tax on the bill | None; tax can't be shown on any part of the charge | On the materials price (or the contractor's cost, if higher) |
| Sales tax permit | Not needed for this alone | Needed, and the contractor files returns |

A guaranteed-maximum contract that doesn't break out materials and all labor counts as lump-sum in Texas.

## How does it work on a Texas job, in numbers?

Take a made-up $200,000 commercial job with $60,000 of materials, at an assumed 8% combined rate:

- **Lump-sum:** the contractor pays $4,800 tax to its suppliers on the $60,000 of materials and bills $200,000 with no
  tax line. The tax is a cost of the job, so it belongs in the bid.
- **Separated:** the contract states materials at $70,000 (cost plus markup) and labor at $130,000. The contractor buys
  the materials on a resale certificate and charges the customer $5,600 tax on the $70,000, which it reports and pays
  to the state. Tax applies to the agreed materials price or the contractor's cost, whichever is greater.

The rate is made up for the example; use the rate for the job's location.

## Does a contractor pay tax on tools and equipment?

Yes. In Texas a contractor pays sales tax when it buys, leases or rents equipment used on a contract, under either
kind of contract, and owes use tax on equipment from an out-of-state seller that didn't charge Texas tax. Separately
stating an equipment charge to the customer doesn't make it a rental to the customer.

## What about consumables like forms, tape and fuel?

In Texas they're taxed when the contractor buys them, because they're used up on the job rather than built in: things
like non-reusable concrete forms, drop cloths, barricade tape, natural gas and electricity. On a separated contract a
contractor can pass consumables through on a resale certificate only if title goes to the customer when it takes
them and they're marked as the customer's.

## Is repair and remodeling work taxed differently?

In Texas, yes: nonresidential repair, restoration and remodeling is a taxable service under its own rule (34 TAC
3.357), while new construction and residential repair fall under the contractor rule. New construction includes the
initial finish-out of a new building and new square footage added to an existing one; moving interior walls within
existing space is remodeling. Check which rule a job falls under before you price it.

## How does California treat contractors?

As consumers of the materials they install, and as retailers of fixtures they install. Regulation 1521 covers lump
sum, time and material and cost-plus contracts. Stating materials, labor or tax separately on an invoice doesn't turn a
lump-sum contract into a time and material contract; under a time and material contract, a contractor who bills the
customer "sales tax" on marked-up materials is assumed to be the retailer of them. Fixtures (built-in items that keep
their identity, listed in the regulation) are taxed as sales by the contractor.

## When does a contractor owe use tax?

When it uses materials or equipment on which no tax was paid: bought from an out-of-state supplier that didn't charge
the tax, or bought tax-free on a resale certificate and then built in under a lump-sum contract. The contractor reports
use tax itself for the period the items were used. See [use tax](/sales-tax/use-tax) for how it's reported, and
[resale certificates](/sales-tax/resale-certificates) for when you can buy tax-free.

## Can a contractor buy tax-free for an exempt customer?

Sometimes, with the customer's certificate. In Texas a contractor improving realty for an organization exempt under
Tax Code 151.309 or 151.310 may claim the exemption on purchases for that job, with properly completed exemption
certificates, and a contract can instead have the exempt customer buy the materials itself. If the comptroller later
finds the organization isn't exempt, the contractor owes the tax, penalties and interest, so keep the certificate and
the proof of exemption with the job.

## What should a contractor's system record for sales tax?

- Each contract's type (lump-sum or separated, or the state's equivalent), set before billing.
- Materials bought per job, with the tax paid or the resale certificate used.
- Use tax due on materials that went untaxed into lump-sum work, by period.
- Tax charged on separated contracts and supply sales, by the job's location.
- Exemption certificates per job for exempt customers.
