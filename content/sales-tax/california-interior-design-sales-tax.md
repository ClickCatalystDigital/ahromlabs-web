## Do California interior designers charge sales tax?

On merchandise, yes, and on some fees and labor. [CDTFA's Publication 35](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub35/designer-fees-and-charges-related-to-the-sale-of-merchandise.htm) says a designer who sells any merchandise to
clients, including samples or finished drawings, generally needs a seller's permit and pays tax on taxable sales,
based on the retail price including markup and related taxable labor and service charges. What decides the rest is
what each charge is for, which is why every invoice line should say it.

| Charge | Taxable in California? |
|---|---|
| Furniture, lighting, rugs, accessories you sell | Yes, on your selling price |
| Professional fees not related to a sale (concept, layout, color schemes) | No, if listed separately |
| Fees related to a sale (showroom time choosing furniture the client buys from you) | Yes |
| Product at cost plus a separate "fee" for overhead and profit | Yes, the whole fee, unless part is clearly professional services |
| Fabrication labor (sewing draperies, cutting covers) | Yes |
| Repair and installation labor | No, if itemized |
| Delivery by carrier straight to the client, at cost | Generally no |
| Delivery to your studio or a receiving warehouse | Generally yes |

## When is a design fee taxable in California?

When it is directly related to selling the client merchandise. CDTFA's example: a designer develops color schemes and
alternative looks before the client buys anything, and that fee isn't taxable; once the client orders merchandise from
the designer, time spent measuring for draperies, shopping for furniture and accompanying the client to showrooms is
part of the taxable sale. CDTFA admits the line is not always easy to draw and points designers to its Customer
Service Center. Billing product at cost and adding a separate fee covering overhead and profit makes the whole fee
taxable unless part of it is clearly for nontaxable professional services; [how to charge for interior
design](/interiors/how-to-charge-for-interior-design) compares markup and fee structures.

## How is labor taxed on draperies and upholstery?

By whether it makes something or repairs and installs it. For new draperies, CDTFA says all labor up to installation
(measuring, cutting, sewing, lining, pleating) is taxable fabrication labor, and hanging them is nontaxable
installation labor that should be itemized. For reupholstery, materials and cutting and sewing covers are taxable,
while stripping, retying springs and refinishing are nontaxable repair. CDTFA allows a shortcut: bill materials on
one line and all labor on another, and claim 80% of the labor as nontaxable.

| Reupholstery job, labor and findings $150 | Taxable | Nontaxable |
|---|---|---|
| Itemized method | Fabrication labor and findings as itemized | Repair labor as itemized |
| One-labor-line method | 20% of $150 = $30 | 80% of $150 = $120 |

When a workroom does the work for you, ask it to itemize its bill: CDTFA urges designers to insist on itemized billings
from subcontractors, because your own invoice can only split what theirs splits.

## When do you report tax on a client's deposit?

When the goods are delivered or ownership passes, not when the deposit is paid. [CDTFA's reporting page](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub35/reporting-tax-and-keeping-records.htm) says a deposit
for future delivery of merchandise should not be reported until the delivery is made or ownership transfers; its
example is an office furniture order paid partly in June and delivered in July, reported in full in July. That suits
the deposit practice in [invoices, deposits and retainers](/interiors/invoices-deposits-and-retainers): collect the
deposit when the client approves, and the tax falls due with delivery.

## What about built-ins, flooring and work attached to the building?

Those follow construction contractor rules. [CDTFA's real property page](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub35/improvements-to-real-property.htm) says items attached to a building, such as
carpeting, shutters, custom cabinets and plumbing fixtures, make the designer who furnishes and installs them (or pays a
contractor to) a construction contractor for that job; a designer who only supervises a contractor billing the client
directly is not. Then:

| Contract type | Who pays tax on materials | Tax on the client's bill |
|---|---|---|
| Lump sum | The designer, as the consumer of the materials | None |
| Time and materials, materials billed separately with tax and the contract transfers ownership first (or another condition CDTFA lists) | The designer as retailer of the materials | Tax on the materials line |
| Fixtures furnished and installed | | Tax on the fixtures, not the installation |

CDTFA's carpet example: on a $1,600 lump-sum job the designer pays tax on the $800 carpet purchase and charges the client
none; on the same job billed as time and materials with a marked-up $1,200 materials line, the designer owes $99 of tax
on that line (at the example's 8.25%).

## Can a client use your seller's permit?

No. [CDTFA](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub35/purchases-resale-certificates-and-use-tax.htm) says it is not legal to give your permit number to anyone, clients included, so they can buy at retail stores
or furniture marts without paying tax, and that you owe use tax on anything you bought for resale and then used or gave
away. [Resale certificates](/sales-tax/resale-certificates) covers the certificate itself, and
[trade accounts and designer discounts](/interiors/trade-accounts-and-designer-discounts) the vendor side.

## What should a California design firm's system do?

When comparing systems, check that it can:

- Put each charge on **its own line with its own tax treatment**: merchandise, fees, fabrication, repair, installation,
  delivery.
- Tag fees as **related to a sale or not**, so nontaxable professional fees stay nontaxable.
- Support the **reupholstery 80/20** method as well as itemized labor.
- Hold **deposits untaxed until delivery**, then report the full sale in the delivery period.
- Mark jobs that are **construction contracts**, lump sum or time and materials, and treat materials accordingly.
- Total **nontaxable labor** for the return.
