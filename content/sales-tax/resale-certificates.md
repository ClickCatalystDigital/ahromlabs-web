## What is the difference between a resale and an exemption certificate?

| Certificate | The buyer says | Typical buyers of a manufacturer or distributor |
|---|---|---|
| Resale | "I will sell this item, or it becomes part of something I sell" | Distributors, dealers, other manufacturers buying components |
| Exemption | "This purchase is exempt for another reason" | Manufacturers buying production equipment or materials, nonprofits, government, farms |

Both do the same thing for you as the seller: with a valid certificate on file, the sale is not taxable and the tax
is no longer your liability. Without one, an auditor treats the sale as taxable and bills you for the tax you
didn't charge, plus interest.

## Which form do you accept?

- **Streamlined states.** The Streamlined Sales Tax exemption certificate is accepted by all 24 member states, for
  resale and the other exemptions it lists. Not every member allows every exemption on it, and some want a
  state-specific ID number. See [Streamlined Sales Tax](/sales-tax/streamlined-sales-tax).
- **Other states.** Each has its own form or accepts a multistate one: California's general resale certificate is
  CDTFA-230; in Texas, the back of Form 01-339 is the Texas Sales and Use Tax Exemption Certificate.

A certificate is good for the kind of goods it describes. California's guidance is that the property may be listed
item by item or by a general description; if a customer buys something it doesn't normally sell, ask for a
certificate that names that property.

## What must a certificate say?

Each state sets its fields, but they usually include:

- the buyer's name, address and sales tax permit or registration number
- a description of the items, or the type of items, bought tax-free
- the reason the purchase is exempt (resale, manufacturing, an exempt organization)
- the buyer's signature and the date

Texas lists the description, the reason, and the signature and date for a manufacturing exemption certificate.

## Do you have to check the buyer's number?

In Streamlined states, the seller doesn't have to verify the buyer's ID number (the Board notes Georgia as the
exception). You do have to accept it in good faith: a certificate for goods that obviously don't fit the buyer's
business, such as office furniture "for resale" from a machine shop, isn't one. The buyer carries the penalty for
misusing a certificate, and California says intentional misuse can be prosecuted.

## How do drop shipments work?

When you buy from a supplier who ships straight to your customer, the supplier needs your resale certificate. In
Streamlined states, a drop shipper that receives a completed certificate from you is not liable for the tax on what
it delivers to your customer, and the ID number you give may be one issued by another state. Outside Streamlined,
check the delivery state's own rule for drop shipments.

## Where do certificates go?

Not to the state. The buyer gives the certificate to the seller and keeps a copy; the seller keeps it on file and
produces it in an audit. In practice that means each certificate should sit on the customer's record with:

- which states it covers, and which exemption
- the date received, and an expiry or renewal date where the state sets one
- the customer's ship-to states, so an order to a state with no certificate on file is taxed

Manufacturers claiming exemption on their own purchases do the same in reverse: see
[the manufacturing exemption](/sales-tax/manufacturing-sales-tax-exemption), and for exempt purchases later used
another way, [use tax](/sales-tax/use-tax). How an order system should hold all this:
[how an ERP should handle sales tax](/sales-tax/sales-tax-in-erp).
